How do I know when my parent needs help at home?

There's rarely one dramatic moment. Usually it's a handful of small changes that, added together, mean it's time to talk about support.

Most families don't call a home care agency because of a single crisis. They call because they've noticed a pattern — a handful of small changes that, on their own, seemed explainable, but together point somewhere clear.

Changes in personal care and appearance

Skipped showers, the same clothes worn for several days in a row, or hair and grooming that used to be a point of pride now going unattended. These aren't signs of laziness. They're often signs that bathing and dressing have quietly become physically difficult, or that the steps involved feel overwhelming.

Changes around the house

Look at the state of the kitchen and the laundry. Spoiled food in the refrigerator, a stack of unopened mail, or laundry that's piled up can mean the ordinary tasks of running a household have gotten harder to keep up with — not that your parent has stopped caring.

Changes in mobility and safety

New bruises, furniture that's been rearranged to create handholds, or hesitation on stairs are all worth paying attention to. So is a parent who has quietly stopped doing things that require standing for a while or moving around, like cooking a full meal.

Changes in mood and engagement

Isolation can look like irritability, low energy, or a parent who has stopped calling friends or attending activities they used to enjoy. Loneliness and the physical struggle of daily tasks often show up together.

How to start the conversation

Lead with specific observations rather than a general worry. "I noticed the stairs seem harder for you lately" opens a conversation more easily than "I think you need help." Most people respond better to a conversation about a particular moment than to a broad judgment about their capability.

It also helps to frame support as protecting independence rather than replacing it. The goal of personal care isn't to take over — it's to make it possible for your parent to keep living the way they want to, safely.

What this can look like with Kinbound

If some of this sounds familiar, personal care may be worth a conversation. We start by listening to what's actually going on day to day, then match a caregiver whose experience and approach fit your parent specifically.
